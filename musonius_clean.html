Musonius Rufus - Discourses and Fragments

Musonius Rufus: Discourses and Fragments

I. That We Should Not Use Many Proofs for a Single Matter

When a discussion once arose concerning the proofs that young men ought to hear from philosophers to truly grasp what they are learning, Musonius said that it is not proper to seek a multitude of proofs for every single matter, but rather proofs that are effective and clear.

“For just as a doctor is not to be praised if he prescribes a multitude of medicines to his patients,” he said, “but rather if he effectively helps them with just a few; so too, the philosopher who teaches his listeners by means of countless proofs is not superior to the one who leads them to the desired conclusion using only a few. Indeed, the more intelligent the listener is, the fewer proofs he will require, and the more quickly he will agree with the main point of the argument, provided it is sound. But the person who constantly demands proof everywhere, even when things are obvious, and who wants things that can be proven briefly to be demonstrated at great length, is completely absurd and slow to learn.”

He pointed out that human beings, unlike the gods, need proofs to understand what is not immediately obvious. However, because some people are sharper and others duller, and some have been raised in better habits while others in worse, those with a weaker nature require more proofs and greater effort to accept these teachings and be molded by them.

“We can easily understand this,” he continued, “if we imagine two young men. One has been raised in total luxury, his body softened and his soul weakened by habits that lead to indulgence, making his nature sluggish and slow to learn. The other has been raised in a somewhat Spartan manner, unaccustomed to luxury, trained to endure hardship, and receptive to sound reasoning. If we place these two young men before a philosopher who is arguing that death, hardship, and poverty are not evils, and conversely, that life, pleasure, and wealth are not goods, will they both accept the arguments in the same way? Not at all. The sluggish one will agree only with great difficulty and slowness, as if being pried open by a thousand arguments. The other, however, will accept the teachings quickly and readily as something natural and fitting for him, requiring neither many proofs nor excessive effort.”

“Was this not the case with that Spartan boy who asked Cleanthes the philosopher if hardship was a good? That boy was so well-endowed by nature and so well-trained in virtue that he was closer to considering hardship a good than an evil. He asked the question assuming it was already agreed that hardship was not an evil! Therefore, I say that the philosopher-teacher should not seek to overwhelm his students with a multitude of words and proofs, but should speak to the point, reach the mind of the listener, and above all, back up his teachings by living consistently with what he says. The student, in turn, should not seek to hear many proofs for the truth, but clear ones, and whatever he is convinced is true, he must follow in his daily life. For only in this way will anyone truly benefit from philosophy.”

II. That Man is Born with an Inclination Toward Virtue

Musonius used to state that all human beings are naturally formed to live nobly and without sin. A great proof of this is that lawgivers lay down commands and prohibitions equally for all people, without exempting anyone—young or old, strong or weak—from punishment if they do wrong.

If virtue were something entirely artificial, added from the outside without any natural foundation in us, no one would expect an untrained person to be faultless in daily life, just as no one expects someone who has never studied medicine to practice as a doctor or someone who has never studied music to play the lyre. Yet in the art of living, everyone expects even untrained people not to do wrong. This is because human beings are naturally created for virtue.

Furthermore, another proof of our natural capacity for virtue is that everyone talks about themselves as if they possessed virtue and were good. If you ask anyone whether they are foolish or wise, no one will admit to being foolish. Nor will anyone admit to being unjust or shameless. Everyone claims to possess virtue, even if they cannot name a teacher or any formal training. Why is this? Because an innate inclination toward noble character and a seed of virtue exists within the soul of every human being.

III. That Women Too Should Study Philosophy

When someone asked Musonius whether women also ought to study philosophy, he began to teach as follows:

Women have received from the gods the very same reasoning capacity as men—the capacity we use in our dealings with one another and in evaluating whether something is good or bad, noble or shameful. Women also possess the exact same senses as men: sight, hearing, smell, and the rest. Likewise, both have the same body parts.

Moreover, a natural desire and affinity for virtue exists not only in men, but in women as well. Women are naturally pleased by noble and just deeds, and repelled by their opposites. Since this is so, why should it be fitting for men to seek how to live well—which is what studying philosophy is—and not for women? Is it because it is fitting for men to be good, but not for women?

Let us consider the qualities required of a good woman: she must be skilled in household management, capable of evaluating what benefits her family, and competent in guiding servants. Philosophy provides these very qualities, for philosophy is nothing other than the science of life. Furthermore, a woman must be self-controlled, free from unlawful desires, not controlled by anger or grief, and superior to all irrational passions. These are precisely the teachings of philosophy.

A woman who studies philosophy will also be just, a blameless life-partner, a good caretaker of her husband and children, and free from greed. She will possess courage, so that neither fear of death nor hardship will drive her to anything shameful. Far from making women arrogant or neglectful of their home duties, true philosophy teaches self-restraint, modest speech, and active diligence.

IV. Should Daughters Be Educated the Same as Sons?

Once when asked whether daughters should receive the same education as sons, Musonius remarked that trainers of horses and dogs do not educate males and females differently. Female dogs are taught to hunt just like males, and mares receive the same training as stallions. Why then should human males require a distinct training and upbringing from females, as though men and women did not need the same virtues?

Both man and woman must possess understanding, justice, self-control, and courage. A man cannot be a good citizen if he is unjust, nor can a woman manage a household well if she lacks justice. Likewise, self-control is necessary for both, as the laws penalize adultery and intemperance equally in men and women.

Courage too is required of women. A noble woman must be free from cowardice, yielding neither to pain nor to fear, lest she be forced into dishonor. Women must be able to defend themselves and their children, just as female birds fight larger creatures for their young. If women seem lacking in courage or physical prowess, this is due to lack of practice rather than natural inability.

Therefore, since the virtues of men and women are identical, their education from childhood must be the same. Both must be taught what is good and bad, helpful and harmful, noble and shameful. Both must be trained to endure pain, disdain death, honor equality, and love justice. Without philosophy, neither a man nor a woman can be properly educated.

V. Which is More Effective, Practice or Theory?

When the question arose whether practice (habit) or theory (reasoned argument) is more effective for acquiring virtue, Musonius maintained that practice is more powerful.

He illustrated this by asking: “If there were two physicians, one able to speak brilliantly about medical theory but having no experience treating the sick, and the other unable to speak well but accustomed to curing patients according to sound principles, which would you choose if you were ill?” The listener replied that he would choose the one experienced in healing.

Musonius offered further examples: a ship’s pilot who has navigated many voyages is preferred over one who merely lectures on navigation, and a practicing musician is preferred over a theorist who cannot play an instrument.

Likewise, in self-control and virtue, it is far better to act with temperance than merely to lecture on it. Theory teaches us what ought to be done, but practice establishes the power to act. Practice has precedence over mere theory because it directly leads a human being to action.

VI. On Training

Musonius constantly urged his companions toward practical training. Virtue is not a purely theoretical science, but a practical art like medicine or music. Just as a musician must practice his principles, so a good man must labor diligently to apply his philosophical lessons.

Because human beings consist of both soul and body, training must care for both. Training common to both body and soul involves habituating ourselves to cold, heat, thirst, hunger, plain food, hard beds, abstinence from pleasures, and patience under hardship. Through these practices, the body becomes sturdy and capable, while the soul is strengthened in courage and self-control.

The training peculiar to the soul consists in keeping proofs ready at hand regarding apparent goods and evils—recognizing that pain, poverty, and death are not real evils, and that wealth and luxury are not real goods. By breaking bad habits formed in childhood and actively practicing virtue, we align our lives with sound principles.

VII. That Hardship Should Be Despised

To face hardships willingly for the sake of virtue, it is helpful to reflect on how much people endure for unworthy ends. Lovers suffer immensely for foolish passions, traders toil for money, and others undergo great misery for fleeting fame. Acrobats risk their lives walking on high ropes or jumping over swords for a small wage.

If people endure such trials voluntarily for useless ends, is it not shameful if we refuse to suffer hardships for the sake of virtue, happiness, and freedom from vice?

Fighting cocks and quail endure pain unto death without understanding justice or nobility. How much more should human beings endure hardship when working to assist friends, defend our families, or become good and just? Everything good is acquired through effort, and he who refuses to labor judges himself unworthy of noble things.

VIII. That Kings Also Should Study Philosophy

When a king from Syria visited Musonius, the philosopher told him: “Do not think that anyone has a greater duty to study philosophy than a king. The primary duty of a king is to protect and benefit his subjects. To do this, he must know what is good and bad, advantageous and harmful.”

A king must administer justice so that no one receives more or less than they deserve. But how can anyone administer justice if he does not understand what justice is? Only philosophy teaches true justice, self-control, and moral wisdom.

Furthermore, a king must be fearless and courageous, viewing death and pain as no evils. Philosophy alone instills this conviction. A king who studies philosophy becomes a “living law,” creating harmony, imitating Zeus, and proving himself truly fit to rule.

IX. That Exile Is Not an Evil

Hearing an exile lamenting his fate, Musonius consoled him: “How can any sensible person feel burdened by exile? Exile does not deprive us of water, earth, air, the sun, or the stars, nor does it cut us off from human society.”

The entire universe is the common fatherland of all human beings. Being banished from one city is merely like moving to another house. Exile cannot deprive anyone of courage, justice, wisdom, or self-control.

In fact, exile has benefited many people by providing leisure for study and freeing them from political duties and false friends. Diogenes became a philosopher because of exile. Great men like Aristides, Hermodorus, and Themistocles endured exile. Exile does not cause poverty or bad repute to a good man; the only real evil is vice.

X. Will the Philosopher Prosecute Anyone for Personal Injury?

Musonius stated that a philosopher will never file a lawsuit for personal injury (hybris), nor advise anyone else to do so. Insults, blows, or insults do not truly harm or disgrace the sufferer, but rather the doer.

When Spartan boys were publicly whipped, they took pride in their endurance. Socrates was mocked publicly by Aristophanes and remained unbothered. Phocion calmly forgave a man who insulted his wife.

To seek revenge or bite back at someone who wrongs you is the behavior of a wild animal, not a rational human being. Most wrongs are committed out of ignorance. A philosopher shows greatness of mind by forgiving offenses and offering goodwill in return.

XI. What Means of Livelihood Is Appropriate for a Philosopher?

For a physically strong man, working the land is the most honorable and suitable means of livelihood. The earth repays labor most justly, bestowing an abundance of all life’s necessities.

Agriculture, shepherd’s work, and planting trees are fitting for free men. Hesiod was a shepherd, yet remained beloved by the gods. Farming provides the mind with leisure to reflect on noble matters while keeping the body strong.

Living in the countryside is healthier and more independent than sitting in the city like a sophist. A philosopher-farmer teaches his students by action, showing them how to endure physical labor rather than relying on others.

XII. On Sexual Indulgence

Indulgence in sexual matters is thoroughly shameful. Only sexual relations within marriage, entered into for the procreation of children, are right and lawful.

Relations aimed purely at pleasure—whether through adultery, relations between men, or other illicit unions—are unlawful and stem from lack of self-restraint.

Even relations with one’s own slave girl are blameworthy. If a married woman having relations with her male slave is considered intolerable, a man should likewise show discipline and not yield to licentious desires with his female slaves.

XIIIA. What Is the Chief End of Marriage?

Marriage is a complete union of life between a man and a woman, aimed both at living together and procreating children, with all things held in common.

Marriage requires complete mutual care and devotion in health and in sickness. Where both partners strive to outdo each other in affection, the marriage is beautiful and enviable.

Where one or both partners seek only their own selfish interest, the union fails and becomes worse than solitude.

XIIIB. What Is the Chief End of Marriage?

Those who marry should look neither to noble lineage, wealth, nor physical beauty. Neither wealth nor beauty fosters harmony or produces good children.

For marriage, healthy bodies capable of hard work are sufficient. As for the soul, one should seek a partner naturally inclined toward self-control, justice, and virtue.

Without harmony of spirit, no marriage can be good. Just as two crooked pieces of wood cannot fit together, two wicked persons can never live in true agreement.

XIV. Is Marriage a Handicap for the Philosopher?

When someone claimed that marriage is an obstacle to philosophy, Musonius replied that Pythagoras, Socrates, and Crates were all married, yet were great philosophers.

Marriage is entirely in accordance with human nature. The Creator divided our race into two sexes and instilled in them a powerful mutual desire so that they might live together, build homes, and preserve the human race.

Whoever abolishes marriage destroys the home, the city, and the human race. A loving wife is a man’s closest companion, lightening his griefs and doubling his joys. Therefore, marriage is not a handicap, but a natural duty for the philosopher.

XV. Should Every Child That Is Born Be Reared?

Lawgivers rightly considered a large population to be the greatest benefit to a city. They instituted rewards for large families and penalties for childlessness.

Refusing to rear children or procuring abortions is a sin against the ancestral gods and Zeus, the guardian of the family. A person surrounded by many children receives great honor and respect in the community.

Even poor birds feed their young, so poverty is no excuse for abandoning children. Rich people who destroy their newborn children just to leave a larger inheritance to the remaining ones act wickedly; a abundance of brothers is far better than an abundance of wealth.

XVI. Must One Always Obey One’s Parents?

A young man asked whether one must always obey one’s parents. Musonius answered that obedience means doing what is good and proper.

If a father commands his son to steal, commit fraud, or do something shameful, refusing his order is not disobedience, but self-control and virtue.

True parental goodwill desires what is best for the child. By choosing philosophy and living nobly, a son fulfills the true higher will of his parents and obeys Zeus, the Father of all.

XVII. What Is the Best Viaticum for Old Age?

The best preparation for old age is the same as for youth: living in accordance with nature and virtue. Human beings were created not for pleasure, but for virtue.

Whoever has acquired sound education and virtue in youth will bear the loss of physical strength and youth’s pleasures in old age without grief. He will face death calmly and without fear.

Wealth cannot provide true cheerfulness or freedom from sorrow in old age. Living righteously and understanding true goods and evils is the only genuine solace for the elderly.

XVIIIA. On Food

We should prefer simple, inexpensive food that is easy to procure over costly delicacies. Food produced directly from the earth—grains, fruits, vegetables, milk, cheese, and honey—is most natural for human beings.

Eating meat is a heavier diet, better suited for wild beasts, and it darkens the clarity of the soul. Heraclitus rightly said: “A dry light makes the wisest soul.”

We ought to eat to nourish the body, not to indulge in gluttony. Elaborate culinary art destroys health, whereas simple food produces strength and endurance.

XVIIIB. On Food

Gluttony is thoroughly shameful. Pleasure in eating is the most difficult passion to control because we must encounter it every day of our lives.

Vices in eating include eating too much, eating too fast, getting messily smeared with sauces, preferring rich dainties over healthy food, and taking more than one’s fair share.

God created food for preservation and health, not for mere pleasure. Those who live on simple food are stronger, healthier, and better able to endure cold, heat, and hard labor.

XIX. On Clothing and Shelter

Clothing and footwear should serve as protection for the body, not for display. Just as the best armor is that which protects best, the best clothing is that which provides utility rather than attracting idle glances.

Excessive wrapping and soft garments weaken the body. Going barefoot and wearing a single simple garment strengthens the feet and body.

Houses should be built purely to satisfy basic needs—protecting against extreme heat, cold, and winds. Expensive stone, golden roofs, and painted walls are useless extravagances. Spending wealth to benefit fellow citizens is far more glorious than spending it on wood and stones.

XX. On Furnishings

Household furnishings—beds, tables, coverlets, and cups—should not exceed basic needs. Ivory, silver, or gold beds offer no better sleep than a simple pallet.

Earthenware and iron utensils are far superior to gold and silver pieces. They are inexpensive to acquire, practical to use, easy to clean, and do not attract thieves.

Foolish people pursue rare and costly items out of ignorance of what is genuinely good. Luxury corrupts both soul and body, begetting greed and injustice.

XXI. On Cutting the Hair

Hair on the head should be cut only to remove unnecessary weight and burden, not for vanity or elaborate styling.

The beard was created by nature as a natural cover and symbol of manhood, just as a mane is to a lion or a comb to a rooster. Therefore, the beard should not be shaved off.

Shaving the beard to appear smooth or soft stems from enervation and luxury. Men who style their hair unnaturally to please women or boys reveal a corrupted, effeminate character.
